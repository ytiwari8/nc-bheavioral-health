North Carolina Behavioral Health

Investment Thesis for SUD/Dual-Diagnosis Residential Facility
Recover Now | March 2026

I. Investment Thesis

North Carolina presents a compelling opportunity for de novo behavioral health facility development based on three factors: regulatory tailwind, supply-demand imbalance, and favorable commercial payer dynamics.

Why North Carolina?

  • CON exemption for behavioral health: As of March 2023, psychiatric and chemical dependency treatment beds are exempt from Certificate of Need review, removing the primary regulatory barrier to new market entry.
  • 68 of 100 counties have zero licensed SUD residential beds. Eastern and Coastal NC have 7-8 beds per 100K population versus 25-35/100K benchmarks.
  • 11 million population with continued in-migration (+1.5% annually, 8th fastest in US).
  • Strong commercial payer concentration: Blue Cross NC holds ~82% of the large group market. Charlotte and Raleigh metros have large employer bases (banking, tech, healthcare) with commercial insurance.
  • Medicaid expansion tailwind: NC expanded Medicaid in December 2023, adding 675,000+ enrollees, though our thesis emphasizes commercial payers.

II. Regulatory Environment

Certificate of Need: What Changed

North Carolina enacted Session Law 2023-7 (HB 76) in March 2023, which included major CON reforms as part of the Medicaid expansion compromise. The key change for behavioral health:

CON Exemption for Behavioral Health

"Chemical dependency treatment facility" and "psychiatric facility" were removed from the definition of "health service facility." "Psychiatric beds" and "chemical dependency treatment beds" were removed from the definition of "health service facility bed." CONs are no longer required for 24-hour drug treatment facilities or licensed facilities for individuals with mental health disorders, developmental disabilities, and substance use disorders.

What this means: New entrants can develop SUD/MH residential facilities without applying for a Certificate of Need, without proving "need" under the State Medical Facilities Plan, and without competing against incumbent providers in a contested CON process.

What this does NOT mean: The CON exemption does not eliminate other regulatory requirements. Facilities must still obtain:

RequirementAgencyNotes
State licensure NC DHSR Required under 10A NCAC 27G. Application includes physical plant review, program review, staffing requirements.
Local zoning approval County/municipal Licensure application requires zoning verification. Residential treatment typically requires commercial, mixed-use, or healthcare facility zoning.

CON Law Status (December 2025)

The broader CON law remains in effect and was recently upheld. In December 2025, a three-judge panel unanimously rejected a constitutional challenge to NC's CON law (Singleton v. State), finding the law serves the public interest. This case involved operating rooms, not behavioral health beds. The behavioral health exemption enacted in 2023 remains in effect and is not affected by this ruling.

Zoning Considerations

Zoning is often the binding constraint for behavioral health facility siting. Key considerations:

  • 6 or fewer beds: NC law (G.S. 160D-907) requires local ordinances to treat "family care homes" (6 or fewer disabled persons) as single-family residences. This provides some zoning protection for small facilities.
  • Larger facilities (16-30 beds): Typically require commercial, institutional, or healthcare facility zoning. May require conditional use permit or special use permit with public hearing.
  • Community opposition: Affluent suburban areas often resist behavioral health facility siting through zoning challenges, public hearings, and political pressure. Working-class and rural areas generally have less organized opposition.
  • ADA/Fair Housing: Discriminatory denial of zoning based on disability status can trigger federal civil rights claims, but this is difficult to prove and expensive to litigate.

V. Payer Strategy

Our thesis prioritizes commercial payers over Medicaid and government programs. This drives county selection toward metro-adjacent areas with large employer populations.

Primary Payer Targets

TRICARE: Based on operational experience, TRICARE presents reimbursement and authorization challenges that make it unsuitable as a primary payer strategy. Military-adjacent markets (Onslow, Cumberland) are therefore deprioritized despite apparent demand indicators.

VI. Risks and Limitations

Data Limitations

  • Licensed vs. operational capacity: NC reports ~16% of licensed behavioral health beds are non-operational due to staffing shortages. Bed counts overstate actual supply.
  • Zero beds ≠ automatic opportunity: Counties may lack beds due to workforce infeasibility, referral leakage to adjacent counties, weak local demand, or failed prior attempts.
  • Catchment is geographic, not proven: Actual referrals depend on network status, hospital discharge patterns, and payer steering, not drive time alone.

Regulatory and Operational Risks

  • Zoning/community opposition: Even working-class communities may resist residential behavioral health facilities. Public hearings create opportunity for organized opposition.
  • Licensure timeline: DHSR licensure process takes 10-12 weeks for application review, plus construction/program review. Total timeline 6-12 months from application to operation.
  • Workforce shortage: NC behavioral health workforce shortage is severe. Clinical staffing (psychiatrists, RNs, therapists, techs) may be the binding constraint regardless of demand.
  • Payer credentialing: In-network credentialing can take 90-180 days per payer. Operating without in-network status significantly limits census.

VII. Diligence Priorities

  1. Payer economics validation: BCBS NC rate confirmation for residential SUD by ASAM level. Network adequacy determination. Authorization requirements and average approved LOS.
  2. Referral pattern analysis: Hospital discharge data from target metro systems (Atrium, Duke, UNC, Novant, Cone). Where do commercially-insured patients currently go?
  3. Competitive intelligence: Existing facility utilization rates, payer mix, and expansion plans. Operator interviews where possible.
  4. Zoning pre-application: Meet with county/municipal planning staff before site selection. Understand permitted uses, conditional use requirements, and hearing process.
  5. Workforce feasibility: Clinical recruiter conversations. Psychiatrist, RN, LCAS, therapist availability in target markets. Compensation benchmarking.
  6. Site identification: Real estate availability and cost in properly-zoned areas. Building code requirements for residential treatment (typically I-4 or R-4 occupancy).

Recommended Sequence

1) Payer rate validation (go/no-go on economics) → 2) Zoning pre-application in 2-3 target counties → 3) Referral pattern/competitive analysis → 4) Site selection → 5) Licensure application
